# Winding today's Dart back to 1967

Dartmouth in June 1967 had today's street plan and today's reclaimed waterfront, so the game's map is wrong mostly at the water's edge, on the railway land at Kingswear, at the Noss shipyard, on one hillside road and in everything that moves. The Embankment was up to 6 m narrower and lower; the timber "station" stood partly on piles over the river, with a roofed pontoon of about 1889 reached by a steel bridge of 1928, and the river boats lay against the quay wall beside it. Kingswear was a staffed British Railways terminus with a manned signal box, an idle goods yard where Darthaven Marina now lies and plain green diesel units on a Paignton shuttle; Noss was Philip & Son's working yard, the same firm owned the paddle-driven Higher Ferry, and Sandquay already had a marina and a 19-bedroom hotel. On the water, five white-hulled motor vessels made up the River Dart Steamboat Co.'s fleet and **three paddle steamers lay idle**: *Kingswear Castle* sold and waiting in Old Mill Creek, *Totnes Castle* with sailing-school sheds on her deck, and *Duke of Devonshire* moored below the Higher Ferry. Ashore, the gasworks had already gone and a "Boatel" stood on its site, College Way was not built until 1969, today's Dolphin was the Royal Union Inn and the buses were Western National. Early June 1967 was dry and sunny after a very wet May, under a waning moon, with the Six-Day War on the placards from 5 June and "Silence Is Golden" still at number one on the 3rd. The removal list in `era1967.ts` is mostly sound, but it needs four corrections and about ten additions, of which College Way, the 1993 tourist office, the Marks & Spencer block and the training ship *Hindostan* matter most. A verification pass has now read most of the cited pages in full. What remains unverified is chiefly the old Kingswear lighthouse, every buoy and light character, the real tide and anything that needs a 1960s map.

## Dartmouth kept its plan, but its river edge, pontoon, north end and shop signs have changed

### The reclaimed waterfront was already thirty years old

*Confidence: high. Every structure is confirmed from full pages; only the exact years differ between sources.*

The level ground the game draws along the river existed in full by 1967. The Harbour Commission built the **South Embankment in 1882–85**, from the Lower Ferry slip to Vavasour's Slip ([DHRG timeline](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/a-brief-history-of-dartmouth/)). The railway and the town could not agree who should pay for the stretch at the railway jetty, so a gap stayed open for five years, and the Boat Float "was finally enclosed c1889" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_railway_station); [British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101218121-the-boat-float-dartmouth)). The North Embankment was built between 1928 and 1932 across Coombe Mud; the ground behind it appears on the 1936 map as Coombe Recreation Ground and was **renamed Coronation Park in 1937** ([Devon Historic Coastal and Market Towns Survey](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth,_Devon)). The game is therefore right to keep the Embankment, the park and Royal Avenue Gardens.

The gardens' furniture needs more care than the first report gave it. The bandstand is a cast-iron octagon on eight slender columns with timber screens and a lead roof. Its listing dates it "Late C19", the Devon survey puts it after 1904–05, and one local site says 1911, so it stood in 1967 on any reading ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197506-bandstand-dartmouth); [Dartmouth Green Partnerships](https://dartmouthgreenpartnerships.org/history/), summary only). A cast-iron fountain commemorating Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee stood there too, but **a new fountain was added in 2012**, so the one seen today must not be copied without a dated photograph ([Devon survey](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf); [DHRG timeline](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/a-brief-history-of-dartmouth/)). The war memorial is a granite Latin cross about 5 m tall on a two-stepped octagonal base ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101449537-dartmouth-war-memorial-cross-dartmouth)). The Newcomen memorial engine was **erected in 1963** and stays, but the tourist information building wrapped round it dates from 1993 and must go ([DHRG, Shops & Businesses North](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-north/)).

The Boat Float's form is now documented. It is a trapezoidal enclosure with walls of coursed limestone and some granite, steps down to the water on the west side and a ramp on the east. The road crosses the channel to the river on **a pair of large cast-iron girders shaped as a flat segmental arch** ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101218121-the-boat-float-dartmouth)). The tidal lock gate at that bridge belongs to the flood works of 1985–90, so in 1967 the channel was an open arch ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth,_Devon)). The concrete kerbs on the east and south sides are 20th-century replacements and probably date from the same works, which is an inference. The listing does not describe a parapet or railings, and the Conservation Area Appraisal remarks on "the absence of railings" at the Boat Float, the promenade and Bayard's Cove today ([Conservation Area Appraisal](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Dartmouth_Appraisal_webfinal-2.pdf)). Which boats lay in the Float in 1967 was not found.

### The 1986 flood works replaced the edge the player perches on

*Confidence: high for the pontoon, its bridge and the station building (full pages, three sources); medium-high for the 6 m figure; low for the look of the old quay edge, which no source describes.*

What the game must not copy is today's river edge. Wikipedia states that "between 1985 and 1990 the Embankment was widened by 6 metres and raised to prevent flooding at spring tides", in a sentence without a footnote, and the DHRG's short history says that in 1986 the South Embankment "was widened by up to six meters" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth,_Devon); [DHRG, A Brief History of Dartmouth](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/a-brief-history-of-dartmouth/)). The Conservation Area Appraisal, which the first report also credited, gives no figure. A 1967 Embankment is therefore a quay **up to 6 m narrower and lower**, without the flood wall.

The station is where the difference shows most. In 1986 "the covered wooden pontoon was removed and replaced by a larger open concrete and steel pontoon", and the same works encased and filled in the station building's pilings; until then the wooden building "was partially jettied out over the river on oak piles" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_railway_station)). The first report called the lost pontoon the original. It was the second: when the embankment gap was closed, the railway provided **a jetty 13 ft 6 in wide and 78 ft long (4.1 m by 23.8 m) leading to a covered pontoon 70 ft by 30 ft (21.3 m by 9.1 m)**. In 1928 steel pontoon bridges made by John Lysaght & Co. of Bristol replaced the older bridges on both the Dartmouth and Kingswear sides; they were wide enough for a vehicle and had low walkways along the sides for foot passengers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_railway_station); [Smart, Harbour Papers IV](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/harbour-papers-part-iv-the-ferries/)).

The station building itself opened on 31 March 1890, replacing the original "box on a pontoon"; the listing's "c1864-70" is best treated as an error, since Smart and Wikipedia agree on 1889–90 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_railway_station); [Smart](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/harbour-papers-part-iv-the-ferries/)). The listing gives artists what they need: a single-storey, six-bay, painted timber-framed building on a brick plinth; a canopy with a timber valance on cast-iron brackets, roofed in corrugated iron and glass; a hipped slate roof with a flat top ringed by an ornamental cast-iron balustrade whose standards carry large stars; and, at the south end, a covered approach to the landing stage under a pitched corrugated-iron roof ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101297055-former-dartmouth-station-dartmouth)). The plain modern windows and glazed doors the listing mentions belong to the later restaurant, not to 1967. That the building was a working British Railways booking office in 1967 remains an inference, though a well-supported one: the ferry rights stayed with the railway until 1972, and the building was sold and became a restaurant only afterwards.

The river boats' landing is the other gain. In the early 1960s the Steamboat Co.'s vessels berthed **alongside the Embankment quay wall next to the station, starboard side to and bows up-river**, not at a pontoon ([kingswearcastle.org](https://www.kingswearcastle.org/potm/october-2004-kingswear-castle-on-the-river-dart-in-the-early-1960s/)). The company's offices were upstairs at 8 South Embankment from 1920 to 1969, above the Dart Café and next door to the post office at No. 9 ([DHRG, Shops & Businesses South](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-south/)). The working quay is still the weakest part of the town evidence. Dart Harbour today calls the South Embankment the port's "primary fish and working quay", with davits for landing catch, but no source places the 1967 fish landing, any crane or any coal store ([Dart Harbour](https://www.dartharbour.org/commercial-ships/)).

### The north end had a Boatel, a bus park and no College Way

*Confidence: medium. The evidence is single rows in DHRG's working spreadsheets, read in full and supported in places by the Devon survey and a DHRG book. DHRG calls the spreadsheets incomplete, and no map was seen.*

The first report asked artists to consider a gasworks behind King's Quay. **The gasworks was demolished in the late 1950s.** In the 1960s its site held the **Dartmouth Boatel**, a bar, restaurant and club run by Frank North, which became flats in the 1980s ([DHRG, Shops & Businesses North](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-north/)). Smart's sketch map puts the old works between Clarence Street and the shore, east of King's Quay, beside "Gas House Slip" ([Smart, Harbour Papers II](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/harbour-papers-part-ii-the-waterside-properties-at-hardness-from-kings-quay-to-coombe-mud/)). The same spreadsheet fills in the rest of the North Embankment frontage: a Dartmouth Clinic in the pre-war Coal Lumpers' Rest Room (1950s, demolished 1988), a St John Ambulance station (1950s to 2014), and the former Pinchin Johnson paint works (1930–61) on the site of today's Vavasour House, which became a tool and gauge factory in the 1970s and was probably empty or re-let in June 1967. The strip from King's Quay to Coronation Park was therefore low and mixed, and none of today's six apartment blocks fits it.

Mayor's Avenue was a street of garages and yards. On its west side the spreadsheet lists Cundells' garage and store at No. 1, the upholsterer A. A. van Hoeck & Co. at No. 2, the cinema at Zion Place, the garage of Denning of Dartmouth, a Vauxhall agent, the removal firm Dartmouth Transport and a police station. **Western National's buses parked on the plot where Marks & Spencer now stands**: the depot of 1934 lost its building in the 1940s and the ground stayed bus parking until a supermarket and flats were built in 1995–96 ([DHRG, Shops & Businesses North](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-north/); [Devon survey](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf)). The stone warehouses of W. A. Hawke & Sons, coal and agricultural merchants, stood on the north side, built of local rubble with paired loading doorways and old plank doors ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101253862-warehouse-at-sx-8780-5155-western-warehouse-of-hawke-and-sons-dartmouth)). On the east side the Newcomen engine house stood alone.

One whole road is missing from 1967. The "Outer Relief Road" from Coombe Road up to Townstal was built in 1969, starting in March with the widening of Coombe Road by 7 ft to hold the Higher Ferry queue, and **was named College Way in October 1969** ([DHRG, Up the Hill to Townstal](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-books/up-the-hill-to-townstal/)). In June 1967 traffic from the Higher Ferry climbed by a narrower Coombe Road and the older streets, and the Townstal toll house, Turnpike Cottage, still stood in the electricity board's use; it was demolished about 1968. The same book dates Redwalls Meadow to 1974, Mount Boone Way to about 1980, the Milton Lane schools to 1974 and the sale of the Jawbones land for industry to 1972. Townstal's one new landmark was the Lord Nelson public house, opened in 1966 ([DHRG, Pubs & Inns](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-pubs-inns/)).

### Names on today's map that are wrong for 1967

*Confidence: medium-high for the cinema, the pubs and the shops (full pages, with DHRG's own caution about undated rows); the Cherub's status and the harbour office are unresolved.*

The cinema on Mayor's Avenue at Zion Place traded as **"The Cinema"**. "By 1966 it was known as The Cinema"; the White brothers of Newton Abbot had dropped the name Maxime, and the 1968 *Kine & TV Year Book* lists it so ([Cinema Treasures](https://cinematreasures.org/theaters/29900); [DHRG, Dartmouth Cinema](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-cinema-a-brief-history/)). The local paper called it "Dartmouth Cinema" in 1969. It became the Royalty in April 1973, closed in or after August 1986, was demolished in 1987 and gave way to the present Dartmouth Clinic in 1990 ([DHRG, Shops & Businesses North](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-north/)).

The first report's statement that the Cherub was a private members' club in 1967 is **not confirmed and is partly contradicted**. The listing says the house was "restored c1958 for Cresswell Mullet", the DHRG's pubs history says it "became a freehouse and restaurant" in 1972, and the DHRG's street directory has "Cherub Inn, public house, Frank Mullet" at 13 Higher Street under 1966 ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101209644-the-cherub-dartmouth); [DHRG, Pubs & Inns](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-pubs-inns/); [DHRG, Shops & Businesses Central](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-central/)). The working position is to sign it THE CHERUB and to show it neither as a brewery-tied pub nor as a "free house".

Several pub names on today's map are later. Today's Dolphin in Market Street was the **Royal Union Inn**, owned by Plymouth Breweries; it was the Country House Inn in the 1980s and "reverts to The Dolphin" only in 1994. Today's Market House Inn was the **Market Inn**. Today's Windjammer was probably still the **Commercial Hotel**. The Royal Castle was kept by Mr and Mrs Powell from 1950 to 1970. The Globe Inn in Smith Street was about to close, since the paper said in 1968 that it had "closed some months ago". The Raleigh Hotel at 5 South Embankment and the Queens Hotel at No. 6 were both trading, as was Harry's Bar in Fairfax Place, which was joined to the Raleigh by a passage. The Seven Stars, the Dartmouth Arms, the Seale Arms, the Ship in Dock, the Floating Bridge at Sandquay and the George and Dragon in Mayor's Avenue all traded under today's names ([DHRG, Pubs & Inns](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-pubs-inns/)).

Three public bodies need period names. The town was a municipal borough until 1974 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth,_Devon)). The harbour was not run by "Dart Harbour", which was formed in 1975 by merging two older bodies; their exact titles vary between sources and are discussed with the buoys below. Where the harbour office stood in 1967 is still unknown: the DHRG directory records only later premises, and the "Harbour Office" in the game's data at (−302, 186) is the modern one. The museum had been upstairs in the Butterwalk since 1954, and a second, Town Council museum in Anzac Street stayed open until 1976 ([Dartmouth Museum](https://www.dartmouthmuseum.org/about/)). The post office at 9 South Embankment was built in 1908 on DHRG's evidence and "c1890" on the listing's; it was the General Post Office in 1939 and offices by 1994, and no page dates the move, so "still the post office in 1967" stays a low-confidence inference ([DHRG, Shops & Businesses South](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-south/); [British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197576-the-old-post-office-dartmouth)).

The shop-name gap is largely closed. The table gathers the DHRG entries that are dated to the 1960s or bracket 1967; the year in brackets is the year of the directory, advertisement or memory DHRG used, so an entry dated only 1958 or 1969 is probable, not certain ([Central](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-central/); [South](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-south/); [North](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-north/)). ### Listings give materials and outlines, and a few colours

*Confidence: high for fabric and dates (list entries read in full). The entries describe the buildings as seen between 1985 and 1994, so paint and shopfronts are not proven for 1967. Street surfaces and most facade colours are still unsourced.*

The landmark buildings looked in 1967 much as they do now, and the list entries now give enough to model them. The **Royal Castle Hotel** is a pair of merchants' houses dated 1639 behind a stucco front of four storeys and attics, with two large curving bay windows of the late 18th century on the first floor and, from the "extensive alterations in 1840", a castellated parapet with machicolation and end turrets for flagpoles ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101218065-royal-castle-hotel-dartmouth)). The **Butterwalk**, dated 1635 and 1640, stands on an arcade of eleven granite piers, with carved timber framing above, slate-hanging on Nos 6 and 8, plaster on Nos 10 and 12 and mid-20th-century shopfronts; it had "a major renovation programme in the 1950s after severe bomb blast damage in 1943" ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197516-the-butterwalk-dartmouth)). **St Saviour's** has a "tall unbuttressed west tower, its upper stage from the C17 with embattled parapet and corner pinnacles" and large 19th-century clock faces, so the "battlemented west tower" in `SOURCES.md` is verified ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101293197-church-of-st-saviour-dartmouth)). The **Guildhall** in Victoria Road is dated 1849, in exposed local limestone rubble with Bath stone dressings and a slate roof, with ornate cast-iron railings to its balcony and steps ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101218299-the-guildhall-and-attached-boundary-wall-to-west-dartmouth)). The **Pannier Market** of 1828, in brown local sandstone with granite arcades around a cobbled square, was renovated about 1975, so 1967 shows its earlier state ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101297094-the-pannier-market-buildings-dartmouth)). **Britannia Royal Naval College**, of 1899–1905 by Sir Aston Webb, is Flemish-bond brick with Portland stone dressings, slate roofs and a large stone cupola with a clock ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101208626-britannia-royal-naval-college-main-complex-and-attached-walls-dartmouth)).

Bayard's Cove changes most in the detail. The first report advised marking the Old Custom House's date of 1739 as unverified and modelling it in bare brick. The listing settles both points the other way: No. 4 was "built 1737-39", "the date 1739 is painted below" its door hood, the walls are **painted Flemish-bond brick**, an extra storey was added about 1930 and the building became a house only about 1985, so in 1967 it was a three-storey building in Customs use ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197571-the-old-custom-house-dartmouth)). Next door, No. 3 is listed as "Custom House" and carried, when seen in 1994, a painted board reading "H.M. Customs and Excise" under painted Royal Arms ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101208079-custom-house-dartmouth)). Whether the brick was painted in 1967 is not stated. The fort is local limestone rubble with eleven splayed gunports and a mostly collapsed parapet, the quay is "old cobbled quayside" above a vertical stone wall, and granite steps go down to the water opposite No. 7 ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101208168-bearscove-castle-dartmouth); [British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197533-quay-walls-dartmouth)). The cove was a quiet quay in 1967: the BBC's *The Onedin Line*, which dressed it as Liverpool docks, ran from 1971 to 1980 ([Explore Dartmouth](https://exploredartmouth.co.uk/dartmouth-film-tv-locations/), summary only; the dates were confirmed on Wikipedia in the verification pass).

Three cautions apply to artists working from present-day photographs. The house at 5 Higher Street was "severely damaged by fire" in 2010, and the frontages there and in Fairfax Place were rebuilt to match; in 1967 it was a timber front of about 1635, with two tiers of oriel windows and a slate-hung gable, that had come through a "major repair programme c1960" ([Conservation Area Appraisal](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Dartmouth_Appraisal_webfinal-2.pdf); [RIBA](https://find-an-architect.architecture.com/bbh-chartered-architects-limited/dartmouth/restoration-of-fairfax-place), summary only; [British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101209616-5-higher-street-dartmouth)). The north-west end of Duke Street, Nos 14 to 24, was bombed in 1943 and rebuilt plainly in the 1950s and 1960s, so it should not match the restored Butterwalk beside it ([DHRG, Shops & Businesses Central](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-central/)). And the South Embankment's "Elizabethan" group of 1888–93 has real colour in its listings: grey limestone rubble with red brick at York House, framing panels filled with slates "alternately grey and purple" at 1 The Quay, and painted red brick at the Raleigh ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197512-york-house-dartmouth); [British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197585-1-the-quay-dartmouth); [British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197575-raleigh-court-raleigh-court-and-the-raleigh-public-house-the-raleigh-public-house-dartmouth)). Beyond those, the Appraisal says only that local rubble runs "from grey to brown" and is often rendered or painted. No source gives a 1967 facade palette or says which streets were setts and which tarmac.

### A borough of 5,758, with no regatta bunting in June

*Confidence: high for the census figure and the regatta's month (full pages); the character sketch is this report's reading of the directories.*

The 1961 census counted **5,758 people** in Dartmouth, and the 1971 census 5,707 ([DHRG, Up the Hill to Townstal](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-books/up-the-hill-to-townstal/)). The street directories show a self-sufficient small town: butchers, grocers, two banks, a Woolworths, a steam laundry in Victoria Road that lasted until 1976, a cottage hospital on the Embankment, garages, the *Dartmouth Chronicle*'s office in Duke Street (recorded there in 1969), and a handful of antique shops, cafés and a yacht chandler for visitors. The Royal Regatta is held on the Thursday, Friday and Saturday around the last Friday in August, so June has no bunting, fair or guardship ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Dartmouth_Royal_Regatta)). No dated 1960s photograph of the town was seen: the 1928 aerial view at Britain from Above and the Francis Frith collection could not be opened, and the 1954 film *From Paignton to Dartmouth* on BFI Player was not viewed.

## Kingswear was a British Railways terminus with a dead goods yard

### The station was staffed and signalled; freight had stopped two years earlier

*Confidence: high for dates and fabric (Wikipedia and the list entry, read in full); low for the June 1967 track plan, which needs a map or a signalling diagram that nobody could open.*

Kingswear station opened on 16 August 1864. Its listing describes a single-storey building of **painted horizontal timber boarding on a rendered plinth** under a hipped corrugated-asbestos roof, with a flat-roofed wooden entrance canopy that has a moulded cornice and a zigzag fascia. Behind it stands a timber train shed with boarded sides, timber trusses braced with iron rods and a glazed ridge, and the platform runs on to the north-east under a canopy on iron lattice piers ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101308783-kingswear-station-kingswear)). Wikipedia calls the shed "a rare wooden 100-foot (30 m) train shed" and adds that the platform was extended to 850 ft (259 m) in 1929, that a long public footbridge of 1895 crossed the station to the shore, and that a double-track steel viaduct of 1928 at Hoodown lay just outside ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingswear_railway_station)). **The signal box opened in 1894, received a new lever frame in 1960 and closed on 20 October 1968**, so in June 1967 it was manned; working semaphore signals are still an inference. The signal-box-shaped building at Kingswear today is not that box. It is an office "in the style of a large GWR-style signal box" opened in 2011, and it must not be mistaken for a survivor ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Steam_Railway)).

Freight had gone. **The wharf closed to freight on 4 May 1964 and ordinary goods traffic was withdrawn on 14 June 1965.** The goods yard lay between the station and the waterfront, on land reclaimed north of the station, with a goods shed on the river side of the train shed and a turntable and carriage sidings at the north end; all of it is now car park and boat storage ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingswear_railway_station)). The Dart Valley Railway, which bought the line on 30 December 1972, recouped much of the price by selling surplus land "at Kingswear, which became a marina" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Steam_Railway)). Darthaven Marina Limited was incorporated on 11 September 1975 ([CorpSignals](https://corpsignals.uk/companies/uk/darthaven-marina-ltd-01226089.html), summary only). The 1967 waterfront north of the station is therefore idle railway ground with no pontoons. Whether the sidings, the turntable and the quay cranes were still in place in June 1967, and whether the platform then had one track or two, is not stated on any page read; rusting track is a fair assumption, since a forum post says only that the quayside tracks were removed "later" ([RMweb](https://www.rmweb.co.uk/forums/topic/179823-kingswear-track-layout/), summary only).

The quay itself was not dead. A river pilot who began work in 1967 recalled that "gas coal to Kingswear had finished in 1963 but that lovely jetty was not finished yet", and ships bound for Totnes were later lightened alongside it ([DHRG, River Dart Pilotage](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-books/river-dart-pilotage/)). A ship-sized jetty at Kingswear therefore belongs in the scene, and a 1960s postcard shows a timber-piled wharf beside the station roof ([Simplon](https://simplonpc.co.uk/DartCarFerries.html), picture). Further north, Hoodown had carried "a short jetty and coal tipping chute" since 1902, and in the war a refuelling jetty and fuel tanks for motor torpedo boats ([Smart, Harbour Papers III](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/harbour-papers-part-iii-the-bunkering-coal-trade/); [Kingswear Historians](https://kingswearhistorians.com/kingswear-history/kingswear-in-the-20th-century/hoodown-and-the-kingswear-golf-club/), summary only). The harbour authority's depot there cannot be older than the authority itself, which dates from 1975–76; the first report's "mid-1980s" could not be confirmed on any Dart Harbour page read.

### Plain green diesel units worked a Paignton shuttle

*Confidence: high for the service pattern, the unit type and its green livery (full pages with dated captions); low for through trains and their locomotives in 1967.*

"Except for peak season trains, most services from 18 April 1966 operated as a shuttle service from Paignton", and Sunday trains "were withdrawn from 24 September 1967, although some were run during the summer of the following year" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingswear_railway_station)). Sunday trains therefore still ran in June 1967. The livery gap in the first report is closed. The 15.58 from Paignton to Kingswear on 4 October 1966 was "an unlined green Derby suburban triple DMU", and a three-car Class 116 set led by power car W50856 was photographed on the branch about April 1967 ([railcar.co.uk](https://www.railcar.co.uk/images/20336); [railcar.co.uk](https://www.railcar.co.uk/images/6366)). The units came from Plymouth Laira and had no gangways between the cars ([railcar.co.uk](https://www.railcar.co.uk/operations/western-region/paignton-kingswear); [railcar.co.uk](https://www.railcar.co.uk/type/class-116/operations)). The train to draw is therefore **a Derby Class 116 three-car suburban unit in plain unlined green with a small yellow warning panel on each cab end**. Rail blue was only just arriving: one early blue car with a small yellow panel is dated to Exeter Central in June 1967, so a blue set is possible but undocumented on the branch, and full yellow ends are too late ([railcar.co.uk](https://www.railcar.co.uk/type/class-116/liveries)). By September 1969 the branch train was a two-car Class 118 in rail blue, which is the wrong picture for 1967 ([railcar.co.uk](https://www.railcar.co.uk/type/class-118/operations)).

Two things stay open. No page gives a last date for steam on the branch. And through trains in 1967 are undocumented: the "Torbay Express" name was applied that year to a Saturdays-only train leaving Paddington at 10.50, but whether it reached Kingswear, and behind what locomotive, is not stated ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torbay_Express)). A Warship diesel-hydraulic in green, maroon or blue on a summer Saturday remains plausible and unconfirmed ([Western Locomotive Association](https://westernlocomotives.com/shop/liveries-of-the-wr-diesel-hydraulics/), summary only).

This matters because the game's vendored renderer draws the opposite. Its own header describes "a GWR-style tank engine and seven chocolate-and-cream coaches, with smoke" on lines named "Dartmouth Steam Railway" (`src/render/railway.ts`). That is the heritage railway, which began running on 1 January 1973 ([railcar.co.uk](https://www.railcar.co.uk/images/17739); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Steam_Railway)). **Greenway Halt**, which is in the game's railway data, **opened in 2012** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenway_Halt_railway_station_(Devon))). **Britannia Halt did stand in 1967**: a platform "less than one coach long", opened on 18 October 1877 at the Higher Ferry level crossing, used in later years mainly by workers bound for the Noss shipyard, photographed with a diesel unit in 1969 and removed as unsafe in 1988 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Steam_Railway); [railcar.co.uk](https://www.railcar.co.uk/images/18273)). The 1969 colour thumbnail shows a short platform with a small canopy and sawtooth valance, white wooden crossing gates with a red disc, a white crossing-keeper's house with a slate roof and a swan-neck lamp. Whether the halt was in the public timetable in 1967 is not stated. The signalling centre at Britannia Crossing dates from 1991 and is not part of the scene.

### The village: a railway hotel, a private castle and house colours still in doubt

*Confidence: high for the listed buildings (list entries read in full); medium for the hotel's use; low for house colours, where two weak sources pull in opposite directions.*

The **Royal Dart Hotel** is an Italianate stuccoed building of about 1850, of three storeys and an attic, with a tower on its north side carrying a clock face and a louvred bell-stage, a splayed two-storey bay on the river front with a cast-iron balcony under a tented roof, and a wing carried over the carriageway to form a covered way to the platforms ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101146644-royal-dart-hotel-kingswear)). In 1967 it was railway property: it passed with the line in 1972 and "was sold by the Dart Valley Railway into private hands in 1979", and it was still listed as a hotel in 1985 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingswear_railway_station)). That it was trading as a hotel in 1967 is a fair inference, not a stated fact. A granite milepost on the Lower Ferry slipway and a K6 telephone kiosk in The Square are both listed ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101108545-mile-post-immediately-south-of-royal-dart-hotel-kingswear); [British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101108508-k6-telephone-kiosk-kingswear)). The church of St Thomas has a short 14th-century west tower of slate rubble with an embattled parapet and a body of 1847 in local limestone rubble ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101108544-church-of-st-thomas-of-canterbury-kingswear)). The Royal Dart Yacht Club occupied its clubhouse of 1881, before the refurbishment of 1974 and the redevelopment of 1991; whether it had a pontoon is unknown ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Dart_Yacht_Club), summary only). **Kingswear Castle was a private house**, restored as a summer residence in 1855 and bought by the Landmark Trust only in 1987, so it should look lived in ([Landmark Trust](https://cms.landmarktrust.org.uk/globalassets/3.-images-and-documents-to-keep/history-albums/kingswear-castle-history-album.pdf), summary only). Below it stands the Kingswear Torpedo Battery of 1940, concrete "finished to resemble random rubble and thatch" on a platform built over an earlier swimming pool ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101391688-kingswear-torpedo-battery-kingswear)).

Colour is still the trap at Kingswear, and the evidence now conflicts. One source says the riverside wharf buildings were "drab looking" grey limestone and were rendered and painted only later ([Bambrick Studio](https://bambrickstudio.co.uk/kingswear/), summary only). Against that, a 1960s John Hinde postcard of the Lower Ferry shows Kingswear houses in cream, yellow and pink and one large waterfront building beside the slip painted light blue, and a second, unrelated colour photograph shows the same blue building ([Simplon](https://simplonpc.co.uk/DartCarFerries.html), picture; [Simplon](https://www.simplonpc.co.uk/RiverDartSBCo.html), picture). Those are a researcher's reading of a retouched card and a small scan. The working position is a mixed waterfront of bare stone and some cream and pastel render, not today's full pastel terrace and not uniform grey, until a dated colour photograph is seen.

### The harbour mouth: the old lighthouse is the least verified landmark

*Confidence: medium that an older lighthouse stood in 1967 (three search summaries agree; no full page says so); low for its shape and position; high for the Daymark (list entry read in full).*

The clearest lost feature at the mouth is also the one the verification pass could not touch. Three sources seen only as search summaries describe the **Kingswear lighthouse of 1864 as a white-painted stone tower about 11 m (36 ft) high, demolished as unsafe in 1980** and replaced in 1981 by today's small white glass-fibre tower; they call it hexagonal or octagonal ([Lighthouse Digest](https://www.lighthousedepot.com/database/uniquelighthouse.cfm?value=1917), summary only; [Lighthouses of SW England](https://ibiblio.org/lighthouse/engsw.htm), summary only; [mycetes.co.uk](https://www.mycetes.co.uk/a/page65.html), summary only). None of those hosts could be opened, English Wikipedia has no article on the light, and no Dart Harbour page gives its history.

Its position still needs a map. One of the three summaries describes the tower "as part of Kingswear Castle", but the position the game's code gives the present light (`src/sim/env/shoreLights.ts`) is about (371, −131), some 800 m north-west of the Kingswear Castle landmark at (963, −666). Dart Harbour's own notice puts the present light at 50°20.81′N 3°34.09′W and speaks of "the main sector light" and "the old sector light" at that one station ([Dart Harbour](https://www.dartharbour.org/news-and-notices/port-notices/2026/01/04/lntm-01-26-kingswear-lighthouse-unreliable)). That is consistent with the light station having stayed put when the tower was replaced, so the 1864 tower most probably stood at the present light's site and not at the castle. It is this report's inference, not a finding. The only older datum is from the 1882 *Channel Pilot*, as paraphrased by Wikipedia: a main light 85 ft above high water and a second fixed light 110 ft (34 m) to seaward and 70 ft above high water, the two leading ships into mid-channel, with no bank named ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Harbour)).

The rest of the mouth is firmer. The **Daymark** is settled by its listing: "Built in 1864. Slate rubble tower 80 feet high. Octagonal on plan, sharply battered with truncated open top", standing on eight stilted piers formed by tall pointed openings, so it is grey slate and 24 m high ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101108546-the-tower-kingswear)). Dartmouth Castle and St Petrox church stand on the west bank. East of the mouth, **Brownstone Battery** was a Second World War coastal battery said to have been decommissioned in 1956 ([National Trust](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/devon/dartmouth-and-kingswear-countryside/the-history-of-brownstone-battery), summary only). The National Coastwatch lookout in two of its buildings became operational only in July 2005 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCI_Froward_Point)). The game data labels that building "Coastwatch Lookout Station" at (2160, −1257); the structure should stay and the modern use should go.

From 1964 she was "adorned with all these on-deck sheds which were put on to provide accommodation for a sailings school"; a photograph of about 1965 shows a black hull, a white paddle-box face with a fan of slots, a big pale two-storey timber shed with house-type windows over the midships and after deck, no wheelhouse and a short funnel stump, afloat in mid-river with the Dartmouth embankment behind her ([kingswearcastle.org](https://www.kingswearcastle.org/potm/october-2015-uk-paddle-steamers-october-1965/)). Her mooring in June 1967 is not stated. The date of her last tow, 9 November 1967, rests on search summaries of wreck sites ([SHIPS Project](https://shipsproject.org/Wrecks/Wk_TotnesCastle.html), summary only). *Kingswear Castle* is the best documented. The company "agreed to sell Kingswear Castle to the Society in the spring of 1967 for the sum of £600"; the legal buyer was Paddle Steamer Navigation Ltd, with the £600 lent by the Paddle Steamer Preservation Society; and she "arrived under tow at Binfield on the Isle of Wight" on Monday 28 August ([kingswearcastle.org](https://www.kingswearcastle.org/history/); [kingswearcastle.org](https://www.kingswearcastle.org/?p=25329)). In service she had a black hull and saloon sides, a white band at the promenade-deck edge, a white paddle-box face with black slots and a buff-yellow funnel with a black top ([Simplon](https://www.simplonpc.co.uk/RiverDartSBCo.html), picture). Three months after the game's date "the decks looked in a terrible state. The steelwork was rusty. The varnish long unvarnished", so she should be modelled shabby, unlit and silent ([kingswearcastle.org](https://www.kingswearcastle.org/?p=25329)). Old Mill Creek also held the remains of the 1907 *Dartmouth Castle*, which "now form a retaining wall in the creek" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Dart_Steamboat_Co)).

The working fleet's colour is now sourced, and the game has it wrong. "By the time of the introduction of the MV Dartmouth Castle of 1948, the motor vessels had their hulls and cabin sides painted white", with wheelhouses of varnished wood and no funnels; the paddle steamers kept black hulls and a yellow funnel with a black top ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Dart_Steamboat_Co)). Colour postcards agree: *Seymour Castle* with a white hull, a black rubbing band, a teak-coloured wheelhouse, a white awning on a pipe frame and a red swallow-tailed flag, and *Berry Castle* the same with red boot-topping; the company's house flag was **a red pennant lettered "R·D·S" in white** ([Simplon](https://www.simplonpc.co.uk/RiverDartSBCo.html), pictures). The game draws its Totnes boat as *Cardiff Castle*, the right ship, in the dark blue of today's operator and with today's superstructure (`src/render/traffic.ts`). For 1967 she is white, open-decked and funnel-less.

How the fleet worked is partly recovered. The company had been owned since 1952 by the Welsh firm Evans & Reid, which explains the Welsh castle names ([Simplon](https://www.simplonpc.co.uk/RiverDartSBCo.html)). By day the boats lay against the Embankment by the station; at night they went to "the moorings on the Kingswear side of the river", moored head up-river ([kingswearcastle.org](https://www.kingswearcastle.org/potm/october-2004-kingswear-castle-on-the-river-dart-in-the-early-1960s/)). Sailings to Totnes were bound to the tide, about 75 minutes each way with calls at Dittisham and off Duncannon; the 1959 timetable had two round trips, and the first departure was possible about an hour after low water ([kingswearcastle.org](https://www.kingswearcastle.org/?p=25738); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Dart_Steamboat_Co)). No 1967 timetable was seen. The season itself was poor: "despite having a modern fleet of five motor vessels, the company continued to decline in the late 1960s", with the *Torrey Canyon* sinking of March 1967, bad weather and unemployment blamed ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Dart_Steamboat_Co)). The rival "Red Cruisers" of G. H. Riddalls are named on Simplon only for 1999, with no colours and no 1960s date, so they should not appear ([Simplon](https://simplonpc.co.uk/Dart_Pleasure_Craft.html)).

### The shipyard was at Noss, and Sandquay was already a marina

*Confidence: high that Noss was at work and Sandquay was a marina with a hotel (full pages, several sources); low for the layout of either in 1967.*

Philip & Son had built ships at Sandquay since 1858 and took over the Noss works in 1918 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_and_Son)). "In 1958 a decision was taken to convert the Sandquay Yard to a yacht marina"; the hotel, "which had 19 bedrooms, was officially opened in June 1961"; and "the Sandquay site becomes a marina in 1965" ([Devon survey](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf); [Dart Marina](https://dartmarina.com/history/); [DHRG timeline](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/a-brief-history-of-dartmouth/)). The conversion removed the unsuitable yard buildings, filled in the dry dock and added single-storey service buildings, while two slipways, cottages, old offices and a hard-standing for laid-up boats at the north end survived until the rebuilding of 2003–06 ([Devon survey](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf); [Dart Marina](https://dartmarina.com/history/)). The marina's 110 serviced berths and the hotel's riverside terrace date from 1994–97, so the 1967 marina was smaller; its size is not given. A mid-1960s colour postcard shows what stood behind the Higher Ferry: a long three-storey cream building with a red roof, a pink gabled shed lettered "…T MARINA" in red, a yellow gabled shed, long grey and cream single-storey sheds, a tall masonry chimney beside a stone-and-brick engine house at the north end, and yachts along the quay ([Simplon](https://simplonpc.co.uk/DartCarFerries.html), picture). The game's documents are wrong in the opposite direction: `SOURCES.md` says "the marinas date from the 1970s onwards", which is false for Dart Marina.

**Noss in June 1967 was a working yard.** The pilot who started that year remembered "a constant flow of ships going to Noss for repairs", with captains "very pleased with the standard of work", and the DHRG timeline has "shipbuilding and repair continues at Noss until it finally ends in 1999" ([DHRG, River Dart Pilotage](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-books/river-dart-pilotage/); [DHRG timeline](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/a-brief-history-of-dartmouth/)). The first report set that against a Dartmouth Museum statement that "the shipbuilding side closed in 1964". The museum page as served says no such thing and names no year, so that conflict is withdrawn ([Dartmouth Museum](https://www.dartmouthmuseum.org/product/philip-son/)). A softer disagreement remains about new building: the yard delivered the Lower Ferry tugs in 1965 and 1966, the Devon survey says shipbuilding "ended in the mid-1970s", the DHRG's short history says the Noss works "only did repairs" without giving a period, and Wikipedia has ship and boat building "discontinued in October 1999" ([Simplon](https://simplonpc.co.uk/DartCarFerries.html); [Devon survey](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf); [DHRG](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/a-brief-history-of-dartmouth/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_and_Son)). Repair work is the safe thing to show; a hull on the slip is plausible but unsourced for June 1967. The yard lay between Noss and Longwood creeks and had had its own railway siding since 1929 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Steam_Railway)). Its look is known only from a pre-war apprentice's memoir: a 100-ton fixed sheerlegs at the water's edge, two steam cranes on rails, sheds "all of corrugated steel sheets", ships "built in the open, on inclined slipways" mostly at the north end, an entrance at the south end by a road bridge over the railway, and a floating dock lettered "Shipbuilders and Engineers" ([DHRG, The Shipyard Apprentice](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/the-shipyard-apprentice/)). That is a guide to character, not evidence for 1967. Coal bunkering should not appear: the harbour's coaling tug *Portwey* was sold away in 1951 ([engineers.scot](https://engineers.scot/news/2021-02-09-portwey-the-history-of-a-west-country-steam-tug), summary only).

### Type 12 frigates, no blue-and-white picket boats, and tankers at Sandquay

*Confidence: high for the squadron's ships and for the dates of the modern college boats (full pages); low for whether a frigate lay in the river in early June and for what the college had afloat.*

The Dartmouth Training Squadron was the 17th Frigate Squadron. In 1967 it comprised the Whitby-class frigates **HMS *Torquay* (F43), *Scarborough* (F63), *Eastbourne* (F73) and *Tenby* (F65)**, 370 ft (112.8 m) long, led from *Torquay* by Captain Ronald Forrest from August 1966 to December 1967 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/17th_Frigate_Squadron_(United_Kingdom)); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Scarborough_(F63))). "There were three cruises a year which coincided with the terms at Dartmouth College", so the ships may have been away in early June, and no source puts a frigate in the river then. Other grey ships are sourced, though: the 1967 pilot recalled "many foreign naval ships", survey ships based in the port, and "constantly tankers bringing fuel to Sandquay for the boilers" of the college, so a small coastal tanker alongside at Sandquay is a documented sight ([DHRG, River Dart Pilotage](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-books/river-dart-pilotage/)). The college had been named HMS *Dartmouth* since 1953 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britannia_Royal_Naval_College)).

The college's modern boats are a period trap, now closed. The picket boat PB7 was "built in 1975 by Perkins of Whitstable, as Number 7 of 8 picket boats", in glass-reinforced plastic, and served the college from 1975 to 2022, so the blue-and-white boats must not appear ([National Historic Ships](https://www.nationalhistoricships.org.uk/register/3866/pb7)). The moored training ship *Hindostan* is the former minehunter HMS *Cromer*, commissioned in 1992, decommissioned in 2001 and then renamed, and she was towed to Portsmouth in June 2023 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Cromer_(M103)); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britannia_Royal_Naval_College)). The game draws her off Sandquay at (−366, 1333) with no era gate (`src/render/dart.ts`), so she must come out. What replaced these in 1967 is thinly sourced: an early-1960s account has "the young midshipmen from the college gaining their first experience of command in sailing dinghies on the river", and nothing else is confirmed ([kingswearcastle.org](https://www.kingswearcastle.org/potm/october-2004-kingswear-castle-on-the-river-dart-in-the-early-1960s/)). An earlier type of picket boat did exist, since *Navy News* reported picket boats racing at the college in 1962 ([Navy News](https://archive.org/stream/NavyNews1962Collection/196208_djvu.txt), summary only). Two sailing schools based on old paddle steamers add school dinghies to the same water.

### A mainly commercial river: coasters, a petrol pilot launch and few yachts

*Confidence: medium for the working traffic (one pilot's memoir of 1967, read in full); low for fishing boats, yachts and mooring numbers, where nothing specific to 1967 was found.*

Dart Harbour's own history gives the proportion that should govern the scene: "In 1975, when Dart Harbour was formed, 75% of the activities on the river were commercial and 25% leisure", against 95% leisure today ([Dart Harbour](https://www.dartharbour.org/about-dart-harbour/history-of-dart-harbour)). The pilot's memoir supplies the traffic. Timber for Totnes came in Danish "shelter-decker" coasters 185 ft long, and "during the sixties most were German and Dutch, and around 160 ft. long". Ships went up to Noss for repair, tankers to Sandquay, and "almost daily" there were "small passenger vessels from Torquay". The pilots supplied their own boat: in June 1967 it was ***Sturdy*, an 18 ft launch with an old petrol engine**, with Brian Riddalls as coxswain, and it served "until Achieve was built in late 1967" ([DHRG, River Dart Pilotage](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-books/river-dart-pilotage/)).

Fishing is still the thinnest evidence. Nothing was found on the 1960s fishing fleet or the harbour authority's launches, and the picture of a handful of wooden inshore crabbers is general knowledge that should be labelled as an assumption. A pre-war memoir names the small boatbuilders Ferris & Blank in Old Mill Creek, Coakers in Coombe Creek, and Lavers and Lidstone beyond Bayard's Cove; whether any still traded in 1967 is unchecked ([DHRG, The Shipyard Apprentice](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/the-shipyard-apprentice/)). No 1967 mooring count exists in the notes. The game keeps 42% of moored sailing boats and about 17% of motor boats, which leaves roughly 165 of the 443 boats in the data. That is a design judgement, not a sourced figure; the 1975 proportion supports thinning the yachts and adding working craft, and it does not fix a number.

### Two harbour bodies, and buoys that need a 1960s chart

*Confidence: high that two bodies ran the river and merged in 1975–76 (full pages); low for their exact titles; low for every buoy and light as it was in 1967.*

"The Dart Harbour and Navigation Authority was formed in 1975 by the amalgamation of the River Dart Navigation Commission and the Dart Harbour Commission" ([Dart Harbour](https://www.dartharbour.org/about-dart-harbour/history-of-dart-harbour)). The titles vary. The Daymark's listing says "Dart Harbour Commissioners", the DHRG timeline says "Dartmouth Harbour Commission", and the first report's "Dartmouth Harbour Commissioners" under an Act of 1951 comes from a search summary of the 1975 Act ([vLex](https://legislation.vlex.co.uk/vid/808182321), summary only). The statutory title should be checked before any launch, notice or cap badge is lettered. The harbour master in 1972 was a Captain Penny; the office in 1967 is unknown.

For the marks, three things are now firm. Today's buoyage did not exist: Wikipedia captions the older scheme as the "Heritage Buoyage used in the United Kingdom before 1977", so today's green and red lateral buoys are wrong for 1967 in colour, and possibly in shape ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Association_of_Marine_Aids_to_Navigation_and_Lighthouse_Authorities)). Buoys did stand at two of today's positions: the 1967 pilot speaks of the "Ledge buoy", the "Checkstone buoy" and a "leading light", with "no radio in those days" ([DHRG, River Dart Pilotage](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-books/river-dart-pilotage/)). And a sectored harbour light at the south end of the town, showing red and green over the shoals, existed by 1882 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Harbour)). The game places Castle Ledge as a green starboard-hand buoy flashing green and gives the Kingswear and Bayard's Cove lights their modern sector characters, all from present-day sources (`public/geo/features.json`, `src/sim/env/shoreLights.ts`). None of that is evidence for 1967. Admiralty chart 2253 in a 1960s edition is the document that settles buoys, lights and the old lighthouse in one sitting. The DHRG holds a "Chart of Dartmouth Harbour" image, but it is hosted where the session could not reach it.

## Streets, people and sound belong to a transition year

### Vehicles: British-built, sparse, and no plate newer than an E

*Confidence: high for plates, market leaders and the bus operators (full pages); medium for the photographed buses and the named local garages; low for Western National's livery, which no opened page states. The vehicle count, paint colours, registration codes and Post Office and AA vans were not re-checked and rest on search summaries.*

"The Cortina was Britain's most popular new car in 1967", interrupting the BMC 1100 and 1300 range, which was the best seller from 1963 to 1966 and again from 1968 to 1971 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Cortina); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMC_ADO16)). On a Dartmouth street in June 1967 the 1100 should still far outnumber the nine-month-old Cortina Mk2, which is an inference from those dates. Great Britain had 14.1 million licensed vehicles of all types in the third quarter of 1967, which supports the game's choice to thin the parked cars ([Commercial Motor](https://archive.commercialmotor.com/article/10th-may-1968/65/what-are-the-most-recent-figures-available-as-to-t), summary only). BMC offered Old English White and Almond Green from 1958 to 1967, which supports the muted palette already in `src/render/population.ts` ([Classic Car Hub](https://classiccarhub.co.uk/austin-classic-paint-colour-codes/), summary only). **The newest suffix letter in early June 1967 was E**: "'E' suffixes ran only from 1 January to 31 July, before 'F' suffixes commenced on 1 August". Plates were black with white or silver characters, because reflective plates "were permitted as an option from 1968"; the first report's doubt about 1967 is resolved ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_registration_plates_of_the_United_Kingdom)). Devon office codes included DV, TT, TA, OD and UO, so a new local car might read "xDV 123E" ([Primoreg](https://www.primoreg.co.uk/history/registration-code/dv), summary only).

Dartmouth's buses were **Western National**, a Tilling company from 1931 and state-owned from 1948, which passed to the National Bus Company only on 1 January 1969, so no National Bus logos or leaf green belong in 1967 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_National)). Its livery is not stated on any page read; a forum calls it a "green Tilling" company ([RMweb](https://www.rmweb.co.uk/forums/topic/60694-buses-in-cornwall-in-1960s-1970s/page/3/), summary only). Bristol K-type double-decker no. 928 was photographed on the South Embankment in September 1966 and withdrawn in July 1967, and a Bristol K6B was photographed at Dartmouth Depot in 1968 ([SCT '61](https://www.sct61.org.uk/index/operator/wn), summary only). The buses stood on the open bus park in Mayor's Avenue described above. Torbay was Devon General territory, and its livery is now sourced: buses "in red with cream relief", open-toppers in cream with red relief, and coaches as "Grey Cars" until 1971; the article does not mention Kingswear, so whether those buses reached the east bank is still open ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devon_General)).

The DHRG directories name the motor trade an artist can sign: Denning of Dartmouth, Vauxhall agent, and Cundells' garage in Mayor's Avenue; Couch & Stoneman, motor agents at 53 Victoria Road and motor and marine engineers running coach tours from the South Embankment; R. J. Rundle of Duke Street, greengrocer, florist and taxi operator; and a garage on Townstal Road, Pits Auto Services, on the site that became the BP station ([DHRG North](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-north/); [DHRG Central](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-central/); [DHRG South](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-archive/dartmouth-shops-businesses-south/)). The Post Office's standard small van was the Morris Minor ([British Telephones](https://www.britishtelephones.com/vehicles/minorvans/023.htm), summary only), and AA patrols used Mini vans from about 1965 ([Honest John](https://classics.honestjohn.co.uk/best-classics/classic-quirks/classic-aa-vehicles/), summary only). Panda cars were in use in Lancashire by January 1966, but nothing places one in Devon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panda_car)). The car and boat shapes in the game remain modern, as `SOURCES.md` already admits.

### Street furniture: two generations of road sign, K6 kiosks and foliage lamp posts

*Confidence: high for the national rules and for the listed kiosks and lamp posts (full pages); the list entries describe 1988–94, so each object's presence in June 1967 is likely, not proven. Lantern type, railings, benches and surfaces are unsourced. The Hansard, fingerpost, postbox and petrol items rest on search summaries.*

1967 is a transition year for signs. The Worboys rules took effect "on 1 January 1965" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worboys_Committee)). In February 1967 a minister said "all regulatory signs must be changed by the end of this year", funds permitting, and in 1970 an MP was still asking why the report had not been fully implemented ([Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1967/feb/01/new-signs), summary only; [Hansard](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written-answers/1970/may/28/worboys-committees-report), summary only). Fingerposts were not affected, on a forum's word ([SABRE](https://www.sabre-roads.org.uk/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1193809), summary only). A level designer can therefore mix new Worboys regulatory signs at main junctions with older warning signs and black-and-white fingerposts.

**Every telephone kiosk is a red K6**, because the K8 was "introduced in 1968" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_telephone_box)). Five listed K6 sites are now known: two adjacent kiosks about 2 m north of the Boat Float on The Quay, one on the north side of Victoria Road, one at Coles Court, one in the castle grounds and one in The Square at Kingswear, each a "square kiosk with domed roof" with "unperforated crowns to top panels and margin glazing" ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101297058-two-adjacent-telephone-kiosks-approximately-2-metres-to-north-of-the-boat-float-dartmouth); [Victoria Road](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101297043-k6-telephone-kiosk-dartmouth); [Coles Court](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101297081-k6-telephone-kiosk-dartmouth); [castle grounds](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197565-k6-telephone-kiosk-in-dartmouth-castle-grounds-dartmouth); [Kingswear](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101108508-k6-telephone-kiosk-kingswear)). Postboxes carry the cipher they were cast with, from VR to EIIR ([Letter Box Study Group](https://lbsg.org/about-boxes/ciphers), summary only).

The lamp posts can now be modelled. The Boat Float's two listed posts are late-19th-century cast iron: an octagonal pedestal "rising to a bell shape", a standard "fashioned as sprouting foliage" and two tapered, fluted arms ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101218145-lamp-post-approximately-1-metre-to-west-of-the-boat-float-steps-dartmouth); [British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197588-lamp-post-approximately-1-metre-to-south-of-the-boat-float-dartmouth)). A second type, on Bayards Hill and at Bayard's Cove, has a large circular plinth, a bulbous base enriched with acanthus leaves, a fluted tapering stem and four curving brackets holding the lantern ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197534-lamp-post-approximately-04-metres-to-east-of-the-newcomen-road-embankment-dartmouth)). **The two posts on Bayard's Cove were "reset" in their present position about 1975**, so they should not stand where they do today. Whether any of these carried gas or electric lanterns in 1967 is unknown; the town had a public electricity supply from 1902 on a weak source ([WPEHS](https://wpehs.org.uk/early-public-electricity-supplies-in-the-south-west), summary only). Market Square offers three sourced props: a granite horse trough with a drinking fountain dated 1898 and granite bollards, market gates with a spiked iron crest and diagonal plank panels, and on the Market Court an early painted board, dated 1839, "prohibiting the leaving of mules on any public highway, market place or slip" ([British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101208308-horse-trough-approximately-2-metres-to-west-of-pannier-market-market-square-dartmouth); [British Listed Buildings](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101197497-the-market-court-dartmouth)).

For a garage forecourt, the 1967 brands found are Shell and BP ([Grace's Guide](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Shell-Mex_and_B._P.), summary only), National, the rebranded National Benzole ([Wikipedia](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Benzole), summary only), Regent, American-owned by 1967 ([Motor Sport](https://www.motorsportmagazine.com/archive/article/august-1967/11/petrol/), summary only), and Fina ([Motor Sport](https://www.motorsportmagazine.com/archive/article/february-1953/22/enthusiasts-directory-no-5-branded-petrol/), summary only). Which brand each Dartmouth garage sold was not found. No source was found for enamel advertisements, shopfront lettering, bus-stop flags, newspaper placards or parking on the Embankment.

### People: conventional dress, a brand-new police force and white-topped caps

*Confidence: high for the naval dress rules (full page); medium for the police, where the formation date is disputed but both dates precede the game; low for civilian dress, which rests on summaries of one enthusiast site and mostly American hair sources.*

Provincial dress lagged London. One source says fashion "was still regional in the 1960s", and that for mainstream youth "everything changed again in the Summer of 1967" ([Retrowow](https://www.retrowow.co.uk/retro_style/60s/60s_fashion_men.php), summary only). A Dartmouth crowd in early June should therefore be mostly conventional, with jackets, ties, knee-length skirts and headscarves among the old and shorter skirts and longer fringes among the young; the specific garments are art direction, not findings.

Uniforms are better anchored. The Devon and Cornwall Constabulary was new. Wikipedia's article on the force says it "was formed on 1 April 1967", its article on Plymouth City Police says that force joined "on 1 June 1967", and the force's own history, as reported in the press, gives 1 June ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devon_and_Cornwall_Police); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plymouth_City_Police); [Cornish Times](https://www.cornish-times.co.uk/news/999/police-mark-50th-anniversary-129458), summary only). Both dates fall before 3 June, so **the constable on the Quay belongs to the Devon and Cornwall Constabulary either way**, and writers should avoid stating the date. An old badge still in wear would read Devon and Exeter Police, the force formed in 1966 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devon_County_Constabulary)). Royal Navy caps had been white-topped "all year round" since 1956. A rating's square rig was "a white undershirt, a navy jumper, navy bell bottoms, a square blue collar with white stripes, a black silk scarf, a white lanyard" and a peakless cap with a tally, and midshipmen wear the white collar patch that dates from 1758 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniforms_of_the_Royal_Navy)). One history says cadets did not frequent the town much, although many of the college's staff lived there, without saying which era it describes ([Naval Marine Archive](https://navalmarinearchive.com/research/navies/britannia_college.html), summary only). What Dartmouth cadets, postmen, fishermen and shop workers wore in 1967 was not found; the National Museum of the Royal Navy's uniform guide is the place to start ([NMRN](https://www.nmrn.org.uk/sites/default/files/2024-08/Research%20Guide%20-%20Identifying%20Uniforms.pdf), summary only).

### Sounds: the Light Programme, beating paddles and no steam

*Confidence: high for the radio and chart facts (full pages); medium for the vessel and vehicle sounds, which follow from the sourced machinery; nothing was found on church bells or gulls.*

In early June 1967 radio meant the BBC Light Programme, on **1500 metres long wave and 247 metres medium wave**, which ran "from 1945 until 1967, when it was replaced by BBC Radio 1 and BBC Radio 2" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Light_Programme)). The changeover day, 30 September, rests on a search summary ([Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/history/broadcasting/pirate-radio-broadcasting-in-1960s-radio-caroline.php), summary only). The offshore pirates were still on air and legal to hear, since the Marine Broadcasting Offences Act "came into force on 15 August 1967" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine,_%26c.,_Broadcasting_(Offences)_Act_1967)). Whether they could be heard in Devon was not checked.

The chart facts in the first report were a week out. **On Saturday 3 June the number one was still the Tremeloes' "Silence Is Golden"**, in its third week. Procol Harum's "A Whiter Shade of Pale" entered the top ten on the chart of 1 June and "reached number 1 in the UK Singles Chart on 8 June", so it is the number one for 8–10 June and a climbing new record on the 3rd ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Whiter_Shade_of_Pale); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_UK_top-ten_singles_in_1967)). Also in the top ten in the first week of June were the Kinks' "Waterloo Sunset", Jimi Hendrix's "The Wind Cries Mary", the Beach Boys' "Then I Kissed Her", the Dubliners' "Seven Drunken Nights", the Who's "Pictures of Lily", Engelbert Humperdinck's "There Goes My Everything" and the Supremes' "The Happening". *Sgt. Pepper* had been "released in the UK on 26 May 1967", ahead of its official date of 1 June ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sgt._Pepper%27s_Lonely_Hearts_Club_Band)). Ice-cream vans traditionally play Greensleeves ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greensleeves)).

The sourced machinery gives the sound designer a distinct river. The Higher Ferry made "the familiar flap, flap, flap of the floats beating the water" over a diesel generator ([kingswearcastle.org](https://www.kingswearcastle.org/?p=17274)). The trains were diesel units, so there is no chuff and no steam whistle at Kingswear, although a manned signal box was at work. The pilot boat had an old petrol engine. The buses included ageing Bristol K-types. The Dittisham ferry was called by a shore bell. All three paddle steamers were silent, and Noss was a repair yard. No source was found for St Saviour's bell-ringing, for gull species or numbers, or for the legal hours of ice-cream chimes in 1967.

The remaining work should run in order of leverage. One Ordnance Survey sheet of the 1960s settles the footprints of the North Embankment, the bus park, the sidings and the lighthouse at once, and it needs only a person with a browser. One Admiralty chart settles the buoys and lights. Seven minutes of the 1964 film settle the Higher Ferry. The 1967 tide table and the Monthly Weather Report settle the conditions. After that, the national background on vehicles, signs, dress and prices is the largest block still resting on search summaries; it is low-risk, because it is generic, but none of it should be printed as a figure or a quotation until the page has been read.

## Conclusion

Reading the sources in full changed the picture in one consistent direction: the river of June 1967 was busier, more commercial and odder than the first, cautious draft allowed. It had three laid-up paddle steamers, two of them floating dormitories for sailing schools; white trip boats tied to the quay wall; a shipyard that also ran the car ferry; timber coasters, fuel tankers and a pilot launch with a petrol engine; and, on the authority's own later reckoning, far more working traffic than leisure. The town matched it, with a bus park, garages and a Boatel where today's map shows a food hall and apartment blocks. The first draft's errors were mostly errors of thinness, and the corrections give the artists and writers more to build, not less.

The priority for building has sharpened without changing. Almost every moving object in the game is still present-day: a 2009 cable ferry, a modern passenger launch, a dark blue river boat, a heritage steam train and a 21st-century training ship. Replacing those, adding the station pontoon and the three steamers, and deleting College Way would buy more 1967 than any further building removals, and each now has a sourced shape and, in most cases, a sourced colour. The chosen week remains rich in things about to vanish: *Kingswear Castle* sold and waiting for her tow, *Totnes Castle* months from sinking, a Bristol K-type bus a month from withdrawal, a signal box sixteen months from closure, a toll house a year from demolition, a hillside two years from its relief road and a radio dial that would be rearranged by autumn. What stands between this brief and a buildable one is now small and specific: a map, a chart, a tide table and a short film, none of which a search engine could read for us.
