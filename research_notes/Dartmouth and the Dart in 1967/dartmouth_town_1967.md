## 1. The Embankment, Boat Float, Quay, gardens, parks and streets: what stood there in 1967 and how it looked

### Takeaway
By 1967 the whole waterfront was already reclaimed. The South Embankment was built 1881–85 (the stretch at the Boat Float was not finished until c.1889). The North Embankment and Coronation Park were reclaimed 1928–37, and the park opened in 1937. So the 1967 layout matches today's in plan. What differed was the detail: the 1967 waterfront came before the 1986 flood-prevention rebuild of the Embankment, and before the c.1975 renovation of the Pannier Market. Royal Avenue Gardens, with its 1911 cast-iron bandstand and 1897 fountain, was already in place.

### Cited Findings
**The Boat Float and the Quay**
- [V] The Boat Float is a trapezoid of water with reclaimed land on three sides; the embankment of c.1889 forms the fourth (east) side. Parts may date from 1585 and the north side probably from c.1685. The major works of c.1889 were by the engineer W. N. Bidder. The engineer's plans and elevations, dated 1879, are on display in Dartmouth Museum. The south and west sides were Dartmouth's main quay from 1585, and the south side was called "Spithead" in the 18th and 19th centuries. — [British Listed Buildings, The Boat Float (Historic England list entry 1218121)](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101218121-the-boat-float-dartmouth)
- [V] Today it is a "sheltered inner harbour connected to the river beneath the road" (a modern description). — [Geograph, Dartmouth Boat Float](https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/8447939)
- [V] Spithead was extended in 1864 when the railway reached Kingswear, and a pontoon was built and linked to Spithead by a bridge. The railway directors and others formed the Dartmouth Harbour Commissioners. — [Wikipedia, Dartmouth, Devon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth,_Devon)
- [V] The Royal Castle Hotel stands at 11 The Quay, facing the Boat Float (see section 2). — [Historic England list entry 1218065](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1218065); [Flickr transcript of listing](https://www.flickr.com/photos/guyfogwill/25225765028)

**The South (and first North) Embankment: date built**
- [V] In 1881 the Harbour Commissioners proposed an embankment or esplanade from near the Lower Ferry to Hardness, across the remains of The Pool. It was completed in 1885 "after much disagreement between the Borough, the Commissioners and the Railway". — [Wikipedia, Dartmouth, Devon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth,_Devon)
- [V] 1882–85: "Amidst much controversy the Dartmouth Harbour Commission builds the South Embankment from the Lower Ferry Slip to Vavasour's Slip to provide for larger ships." — [Dartmouth History Research Group, A Brief History of Dartmouth](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/a-brief-history-of-dartmouth/)
- [V] The town began waterfront improvements in 1884, with a new embankment north and south of the railway jetty. The railway and town could not agree who would pay for the section at the jetty, which left a gap for five years. — [Wikipedia, Dartmouth railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_railway_station)
- [V] The town council and the South West Coast Path trail both say the North and South Embankments opened in 1885. — [Dartmouth Town Council, Coronation Park](https://www.dartmouthtowncouncil.gov.uk/coronation-park/); [SWCP Dartmouth Town Trail](https://www.southwestcoastpath.org.uk/walksdb/411/)
- **CONFLICT (small)**: dates given for the embankment are 1881 (proposed), 1882–85, 1884 and 1885, and c.1889 for closing the Boat Float. Reconciliation: most of the embankment was finished in 1885, and the gap at the railway jetty and Boat Float was closed about 1889. Sources as above.

**North Embankment extension and Coronation Park (reclaimed when? existed in 1967? yes)**
- [V] "Between 1929 and 1935 the Embankment was extended enclosing Coombe Mud and linking up with the Higher Ferry to cope with growing numbers of cars." — [dartmouth.org.uk, Dartmouth post 1860](https://www.dartmouth.org.uk/history/post1860)
- [V] DHRG gives "1928–39" for the reclamation of the North Embankment and Coronation Park. — [DHRG, A Brief History of Dartmouth](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/a-brief-history-of-dartmouth/)
- [V] The Conservation Area Appraisal dates the work 1928–1932. — [Dartmouth Conservation Area Appraisal (PDF)](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Dartmouth_Appraisal_webfinal-2.pdf)
- [V] The site was Coombe Mud, a shallow tidal area north of the centre that had been lined with shipyards. The area was grassed over as a public park. The 1936 map shows it as "Coombe Recreation Ground", and it was renamed for the Coronation of George VI. — [Devon Historic Coastal and Market Towns Survey: Dartmouth (PDF)](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf); [Wikipedia, Dartmouth, Devon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth,_Devon) (reclamation "completed in 1937 by the extension of the Embankment and the reclamation of the mud behind it")
- [V] The park opened in 1937 to mark George VI's coronation; boatbuilding yards had stood on the Coombe mud. — [Mayflower 400, Dartmouth Heritage Trail](https://www.mayflower400uk.org/visit/dartmouth/attractions/dartmouth-heritage-trail/); [Dartmouth Town Council](https://www.dartmouthtowncouncil.gov.uk/coronation-park/)
- [U] The fill was laid in 1928–29 by miners from Wales. Coombe Mud had been a breakers' yard for surplus warships and submarines, and a 2023 radar survey placed the WW1 submarine E52 under the north-east corner of the park. — [This is my Devon (2023)](https://thisismydevon.co.uk/2023/07/08/unravelling-the-story-of-a-ww1-submarine-buried-under-dartmouths-coronation-park/)
- [V] (Modern only) The park today has open grass, a children's play area, tennis courts and a café. — [Visit South Devon](https://www.visitsouthdevon.co.uk/things-to-do/coronation-park-p2750003)
- [V] The SWCP Dart Circuit notes that "the first part of the Embankment was reclaimed as recently as the 1930s". — [Explore Devon, The Dart Circuit](https://www.exploredevon.info/activities/walk/short-walks/the-dart-circuit/)

**Mayor's Avenue**
- [V] The 1885 embankment cut Mayor's Avenue off from the river. A warehouse "complete with hooks for unloading cargoes" survives on Mayor's Avenue. — [SWCP walk 452](https://www.southwestcoastpath.org.uk/walksdb/452/printable/); [SWCP walk 670](https://www.southwestcoastpath.org.uk/walksdb/670/)
- [V] Mayor's Avenue has two listed former warehouses, one possibly of late-medieval origin. — [Devon HCMTS (PDF)](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf)
- [V] The town cinema stood at Zion Place / Mayor's Avenue (see section 2). — [Cinema Treasures, Royalty Cinema](https://cinematreasures.org/theaters/29900)
- [V] (Modern) The Mayor's Avenue car park is the usual start of the town trail. — [South Devon AONB town trail guide (PDF)](https://www.southdevon-nl.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Dartmouth-Town-Trail-walk-guide-2.pdf)

**Royal Avenue Gardens and bandstand**
- [V] The gardens stand on the "New Ground", reclaimed from a sandbank in the river in 1670–80. It was used for recreation and a ferry to Kingswear ran from it. Jasper Bartlett, owner of the Warfleet Brewery, proposed formal gardens with a park, bandstand and fountain. The town council owned the New Ground. A cast-iron bandstand is listed among the historic features. — [Parks & Gardens UK, Royal Avenue Gardens](https://www.parksandgardens.org/places/royal-avenue-gardens)
- [V] The present cast-iron bandstand went up in 1911 and was "considered at the time to be the finest in the South West". The gardens were named "Royal Avenue" because Queen Victoria landed at the New Ground in 1856. — [Dartmouth Green Partnerships, history](https://dartmouthgreenpartnerships.org/history/)
- **CONFLICT**: GoSouthWest says the gardens were created in 1887 to commemorate an 1886 visit by Queen Victoria — [GoSouthWest England](https://gosouthwestengland.co.uk/things-to-do-in-dartmouth/). The 1856 royal visit is well attested (see section 6); an 1886 visit is not attested elsewhere in these results.
- [V] Parks & Gardens and GoSouthWest say the triple-tier fountain was built in 1897 for the Diamond Jubilee. **CONFLICT**: TripAdvisor reviewers say it marked the Golden Jubilee (1887). — [Parks & Gardens UK](https://www.parksandgardens.org/places/royal-avenue-gardens); [GoSouthWest](https://gosouthwestengland.co.uk/things-to-do-in-dartmouth/); [TripAdvisor](https://www.tripadvisor.co.uk/Attraction_Review-g186253-d4058135-Reviews-Royal_Avenue_Gardens-Dartmouth_Devon_England.html)

**Market Square and the Pannier Market**
- [V] The Pannier Market buildings are Grade II, dated 1828, with a renovation around 1975. Market Square was the focus of a wider improvement scheme that went with filling in the old mill pool, supervised by the Rev. Charles Holdsworth using family money. — [Historic England list entry 1297094](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1297094)
- [V] It opened in 1828 as a pannier market, where goods traditionally came in by pony in baskets. It was "not entirely roofed": the ranges form a quadrangle around the former Market Court House, which still stands in the middle. — [Explore Dartmouth, Old Market](https://exploredartmouth.co.uk/dartmouths-old-market/)
- [V] The Market Court House was completed in 1828: two storeys, originally open on the ground floor, "in the middle of a cobbled yard enclosed by the pannier market buildings". — [Devon HCMTS (PDF)](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf)

**Duke Street, Foss Street, Fairfax Place, Lower Street, Smith Street, Higher Street, Victoria Road**
- [V] Duke Street: the Butterwalk is a row of four merchants' houses and shops of the 1630s (1635–40), each Grade I. — [Wikipedia, Dartmouth Butterwalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Butterwalk)
- [V] Duke Street and the Butterwalk were bomb-damaged in 1943, and plans to demolish and to restore them are documented. Oral-history entries recall a Hannaford's shop in Duke Street and Dunnings Bakery in Foss Street (no years given). — [DHRG Social History](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/category/social-history/); [DHRG Shops and Businesses](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/category/dhrg_property/dhrg_commercial/shop-and-businesses/)
- [V] 6 Foss Street is a listed shop damaged in the 1943 bombing raid. — [Historic England list entry 1355046](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1355046)
- [V] Fairfax Place dates from the 1880s and is "a good example of Victorian frontages", which are decorative. — [Conservation Area Appraisal (PDF)](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Dartmouth_Appraisal_webfinal-2.pdf)
- [V] Nos 1–3 Fairfax Place were built in 1880 for R. C. Cranford as one symmetrical frontage. The entry for No. 1 calls it "lavishly decorated in C17 style"; another entry calls it Tudor style. No. 2 has an elaborate timber-framed front with jettied and gabled elements, in stone, brick and timber under a slate roof. — [HE 1297104 (No. 1)](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1297104); [HE 1197520 (No. 2)](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1197520)
- [V] 9 Fairfax Place is 17th century, with stone rubble side walls and plastered timber-framed walls front and back, rendered chimney shafts, a slate roof and a 20th-century replacement shopfront. — [HE 1208855](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1208855)
- [V] A fire in May 2010 damaged buildings in Fairfax Place and Higher Street, and the frontages were rebuilt to match. **Today's frontages there are therefore post-2010 reconstructions.** — [RIBA Find an Architect, BBH, Restoration of Fairfax Place](https://find-an-architect.architecture.com/bbh-chartered-architects-limited/dartmouth/restoration-of-fairfax-place)
- [V] Higher Street: Tudor House (5 Higher Street) is Grade II*, c.1600, with exposed moulded timber framing and a slate-hung gable and attic storey. **CONFLICT**: a photo record dates the timber-framed facade, with two tiers of oriel windows, to 1635. — [Heritage Gateway MDV8542](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/heritage-gateway/results/record/c39bc47e-3b72-4a06-9863-681629e456a2/MDV8542); [Geograph 1033548](https://www.geograph.org.uk/photo/1033548)
- [V] The Shambles (1 Higher Street) has a jettied shopfront with end walls corbelled out at each floor. The second floor has 17th-century close-studding, "probably slate-hung originally". — [British Listed Buildings, The Shambles](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101292572-the-shambles-dartmouth)
- [V] Smith Street: the Seven Stars (8 Smith Street, Grade II, first listed 1972) has a narrow south front with a deep first-floor jetty on corbels. Late-16th/early-17th-century fabric survives behind plaster; it was originally two houses with walls mainly timber-framed under plaster. — [HE 1293160](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1293160)
- [V] Lower Street: its line "was originally the waterfront". The appraisal groups Lower Street in Character Area 3, and Smith Street, Higher Street and Fairfax Place in Character Area 5. Slate-hanging is a notable feature across much of the conservation area. — [Conservation Area Appraisal (PDF)](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Dartmouth_Appraisal_webfinal-2.pdf); [Devon HCMTS (PDF)](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf)
- [V] Agincourt House, 27 Lower Street, is Grade II (listed 1949), of medieval origin: parts of the stone walls possibly 14th century, rebuilt probably in the mid-17th century. **CONFLICT**: Heritage Gateway says built 1415 and restored for Charles II's visit; the Bayards Cove Inn's material says 14th century. — [HE 1209713](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1209713); [Bayards Cove Inn brochure (PDF)](https://bayardscoveinn.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Bayards-Cove-Inn_16pp-Brochure.pdf)
- [V] Victoria Road was built in 1825 as "New Road", so that horse-drawn carriages could enter the town for the first time. It is lined with a group of second-quarter 19th-century buildings, among them the Guildhall. — [British Listed Buildings, The Guildhall](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101218299-the-guildhall-and-attached-boundary-wall-to-west-dartmouth)

**Bayard's Cove**
- [V] Bayard's Cove Fort is a small Tudor artillery fort of about 1510. Fairfax's Parliamentary army seized it in January 1646. — [Wikipedia, Bayard's Cove Fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayard%27s_Cove_Fort)
- [V] The Old Custom House, 4 Bayards Cove, is Grade II (first listed 11 December 1969). It is a **brick** building similar to the Mansion House of 1736, one of a "good group" on the old quay, formerly known as No. 4 Custom House or Trinity House. — [HE 1197571](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1197571)
- [V] A separate "Custom House" at 3 Bayards Cove is listed, and Nos 1–12 Bayards Cove are all listed. — [HE 1208079](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1208079); [Heritage Gateway MDV23634](https://www.heritagegateway.org.uk/gateway/Results_Single.aspx?uid=MDV23634&resourceID=104)
- **CONFLICT / unconfirmed**: the project's earlier SOURCES.md dates the Old Custom House to 1739. This round's summary of HE 1197571 did not repeat 1739; it gave only "similar to the Mansion House (1736)".
- [U] Quay surface: the Bayards Cove Inn calls it "a perfect quay" with cobbles bearing the date 1665. **CONFLICT**: another source says the quay was built in 1539. — [Bayards Cove Inn, In and around Dartmouth](https://bayardscoveinn.co.uk/dartmouth/in-and-around-dartmouth/)
- [V] The BBC's *The Onedin Line* used the cove as Liverpool's docks from 1971 to 1980, which is **after** 1967. — [Explore Dartmouth, film and TV locations](https://exploredartmouth.co.uk/dartmouth-film-tv-locations/)

### Inferences
- In June 1967 the town's footprint matched today's: the South Embankment (1885/c.1889), the North Embankment and Coronation Park (1937), and Royal Avenue Gardens with its 1911 bandstand were all in place. Coronation Park had existed for 30 years.
- The 1967 Embankment came **before the 1986 flood-prevention works** (see section 4). The modern raised walls, pontoon and paving along the South Embankment should not be copied as they are.
- In 1967 the Pannier Market was in its pre-c.1975 state, with a cobbled central yard around the Court House (cobbles are documented for 1828; their 1967 state is unverified).
- Fairfax Place and Higher Street frontages seen today are partly post-2010 rebuilds. They follow the old appearance, but should be checked against pre-2010 photographs.
- Bayard's Cove in 1967 was a quiet old quay of listed buildings and the fort, before it was dressed for *The Onedin Line*. The Old Custom House should be modelled in **brick**, unlike the rendered or slate-hung houses around it.

## 2. Named buildings and their 1967 uses (hotels, pubs, museum, church, Guildhall, Custom House, station, harbour office, post office, cinema, banks, shops)

### Takeaway
Several 1967 names differ from today's. The cinema was **"The Cinema"** (formerly the Maxime; it became the Royalty only in 1973). **The Cherub was not a pub** in 1967: it was a private members' club, refurbished in 1958, and became a pub in the 1970s. The museum had occupied the Butterwalk since **1954**. The "station" was a working British Rail ticket office with a **covered wooden pontoon**. I could not establish where the harbour master's office was in 1967, or the 1960s banks and chip shops.

### Cited Findings
**Royal Castle Hotel**
- [V] A pair of merchants' houses dated 1639, built for William Barnes and Joseph Cubbitt and converted to an inn in the 18th century. Grade II*, first listed 14 September 1949. — [HE 1218065](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1218065)
- [V] It has a **stucco front with broad sashed bays and mock crenellations**, concealing two gabled houses that share a granite party wall, and a 1639 date tablet with the builders' initials. It was united into an inn in the 18th century, first called the Castle Inn in the 1770s, and extensively altered in 1840. — [Heritage Gateway MDV8546](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/heritage-gateway/results/record/c39bc47e-3b72-4a06-9863-681629e456a2/MDV8546)
- **CONFLICT (small)**: the Flickr listing transcript gives 1782 for the Castle Inn name. The English Buildings blog suggests the fortified parapet probably dates from the remodelling. No source supports an "1830s" refronting; the documented date is 1840. — [Flickr](https://www.flickr.com/photos/guyfogwill/25225765028); [English Buildings blog (2021)](https://englishbuildings.blogspot.com/2021/01/dartmouth-devon_19.html)
- [V] The Dart Yacht Club (later Royal Dart) was founded on 8 June 1866 at a meeting in the Castle Hotel, Dartmouth. — [Wikipedia, Royal Dart Yacht Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Dart_Yacht_Club)

**The Butterwalk and Dartmouth Museum (museum since 1954)**
- [V] Built 1635–40 as merchants' shops and houses. The museum occupies the upper storey, reached by a spiral stair built around a ship's mast. Charles II was entertained there in July 1671. — [Wikipedia, Dartmouth Butterwalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Butterwalk); [Wikipedia, Dartmouth Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Museum)
- [V] A high-explosive bomb hit the Butterwalk on 13 February 1943 and the adjacent building was destroyed. — [Historic England archive photo OWS01/17/066](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/photos/item/OWS01/17/066)
- [V] "Seriously damaged by blast in 1943, repaired and reinstated 1954." — [Heritage Gateway MDV24855](https://www.heritagegateway.org.uk/gateway/Results_Single.aspx?uid=MDV24855&resourceID=104)
- [V] The museum was founded in 1949 in the old library on Newcomen Road and moved into the Butterwalk in 1954. The local historian Percy Russell is credited with both the building's restoration and the move. — [Dartmouth Museum, About Us](https://www.dartmouthmuseum.org/about/)

**St Saviour's Church**
- [V] Grade I. The lower part of the tower and the transepts date from the 15th and early 16th centuries. In the refurbishment of 1633–37 the **tower was heightened**, the aisles got new windows and the west gallery was built (dated 1633). The south door is dated 1631, but its ironwork is probably 15th century; Pevsner treats 1631 as a repair date. — [Heritage Gateway MDV8551](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/heritage-gateway/results/record/c39bc47e-3b72-4a06-9863-681629e456a2/MDV8551); [English Buildings blog (2020)](https://englishbuildings.blogspot.com/2020/04/dartmouth-devon.html)
- [U] Britain Express claims the door was carbon-dated to 1361, and repeats a legend that the gallery timbers came from an Armada ship. — [Britain Express](https://www.britainexpress.com/counties/devon/churches/dartmouth-st-saviours.htm)
- [V] The church stands on land reclaimed from the Dart, beside the Seven Stars. — [Great British Life, Seven Stars review](https://www.greatbritishlife.co.uk/magazines/devon/22574839.pub-review-seven-stars-dartmouth/)

**Guildhall**
- [V] Victoria Road, Grade II, one of a group of second-quarter 19th-century buildings on New Road (built 1825). It is still owned and used by the town council, whose committees meet in its council chamber (modern). — [British Listed Buildings, The Guildhall](https://britishlistedbuildings.co.uk/101218299-the-guildhall-and-attached-boundary-wall-to-west-dartmouth)
- [V] Dartmouth was a Municipal Borough from 1835 to 1974. — [Vision of Britain, Dartmouth MB](https://test.visionofbritain.org.uk/unit/10042355)
- I found no evidence that the Guildhall was a converted chapel. The "Old Chapel" (a former Baptist, later Pentecostal, chapel) is a separate building in Chapel Lane. — [HE 1297075](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1297075)

**The Old Custom House and Bayard's Cove**: see section 1.

**The Dartmouth "station" (ticket office and pontoon)**
- [V] It opened with the railway on 16 August 1864: a station without tracks, where passengers booked through tickets and crossed by ferry to Kingswear. — [Wikipedia, Dartmouth railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_railway_station)
- [V] The present building is dated c.1890 and served as the ticket office for the Dartmouth and Torbay Railway. — [SWCP photo tour](https://southwestcoastphotos.com/photo_16708412.html)
- [V] Grade II, first listed 19 August 1974, as "former landing stage for ferry from the Kingswear railway terminus". It is now the Embankment Bistro. — [HE 1297055](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1297055)
- [V] **"The original covered wooden pontoon was removed and replaced by a larger open concrete and steel pontoon during the 1986 embankment works"** (flood prevention). — [Wikipedia, Dartmouth railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_railway_station)
- [V] British Rail's line closed in 1972 and passed to the Dart Valley Railway. The ferry went to the Borough Council, then to South Hams District Council, which sold the service to Dart Pleasure Craft but kept the pontoon. — [dartmouth.org.uk, station restaurant news](https://www.dartmouth.org.uk/news/comments/dartmouth_historic_station_restaurant_roof_repairs)
- [V] Later uses: a restaurant owned by the Holland family (tickets sold from kiosks alongside), then the Station Restaurant, then "Platform 1", and the Embankment Bistro since March 2023. — [CAMRA, Embankment Bistro](https://camra.org.uk/pubs/embankment-bistro-dartmouth-148932)
- **CONFLICT**: the building is dated c.1889 (earlier project notes), c.1890 (photo tour) and 1864 (the station's opening). Most likely the station opened in 1864 and the present building dates from c.1889–90, when the embankment gap was closed.

**Cinema: called "The Cinema" in 1967, not yet the Royalty**

No historical date found. — [Coast & Country](https://www.coastandcountry.co.uk/blog/pubs-in-dartmouth); [Discover Dartmouth, great pubs](https://discoverdartmouth.com/great-pubs-in-dartmouth)

Its date of opening as an inn was not found. — [HE 1209713](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1209713); [Bayards Cove Inn brochure](https://bayardscoveinn.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Bayards-Cove-Inn_16pp-Brochure.pdf)

### Inferences

## 3. Britannia Royal Naval College above the town in 1967

### Takeaway
BRNC (Aston Webb, 1899–1905) is a long Wren-style range of red brick with white Portland stone dressings and slate roofs, with a big stone clock cupola over the centre. It would have looked in 1967 much as it does now. Cadets trained on the river from Sandquay, but the type and livery of the training boats in 1967 are not firmly documented.

### Cited Findings
- [V] Grade II*. Built 1899–1905 by Sir Aston Webb; Edward VII laid the foundation stone in 1902. Flemish-bond brick with Portland ashlar dressings and slate roofs. A large stone cupola with a clock stands over the centre bay, beneath a tympanum carrying the Royal Arms. The style follows Wren. — [HE 1208626](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1208626)
- [V] Other descriptions mention Portland stone outside, Bath stone inside, and Italian marble. — [Easymalc, BRNC](https://www.easymalc.co.uk/the-britannia-royal-naval-college/)
- [V] The college chapel was dedicated on 17 September 1905. — [A Church Near You, College Chapel](https://www.achurchnearyou.com/church/19791/more-information)
- [V] Naval officer training on the Dart began in 1863, aboard ships moored in the river. — [Wikipedia, Britannia Royal Naval College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britannia_Royal_Naval_College)
- [V] Sandquay, below the college, is used mainly for seamanship and boat-handling training. **CONFLICT**: sources give 187 or 201 steps from the college down to the quay. — [Wikipedia, BRNC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britannia_Royal_Naval_College)
- [V] The "distinctive blue and white picket boats had been operating on the River Dart since the 1960s" and were retired at the end of 2021, replaced by eight Sea Class 15 workboats. — [Marine Industry News](https://marineindustrynews.co.uk/royal-navy-replaces-historic-training-boats/)
- [V] The P1000-class picket boats had been carried by destroyers and larger warships before serving at the college. — [Wikipedia, P1000-class picket boat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P1000-class_picket_boat)
- [V] Captain John E. L. Martin commanded the college from September 1963 to August 1966. — [Wikipedia, BRNC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britannia_Royal_Naval_College)
- [U] Dartmouth's public electricity supply began in October 1902, from the Urban Electric Supply Co. at Sand Quay. — [WPEHS, Early public electricity supplies in the South West](https://wpehs.org.uk/early-public-electricity-supplies-in-the-south-west); [BIAS Journal 16 (PDF)](https://b-i-a-s.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/BIAS_Journal_16_EARLY_ELECTRICITY_IN_SW.pdf)

### Inferences
- From the town, BRNC is a red-brick-and-white-stone palace on the hill to the north-west, with the clock cupola as its landmark. The building had not changed since 1905.
- Visible activity in 1967 would be cadets in uniform, and boat training (picket boats, motor whalers and pulling boats) off Sandquay. "Since the 1960s" does **not** prove that the blue-and-white P1000 picket boats were there in 1967 (unverified).

### Gaps
- Cadet numbers in 1967, uniforms, daily routine, and what was moored at Sandquay in 1967 (types and colours: grey naval paint or blue and white): not found.
- Whether the Dartmouth Training Squadron's frigates lay in the river in June 1967 belongs to the ships researcher's scope, but it affects the backdrop.

## 4. The waterfront in 1967: landing stages, pontoons, slipways, ferry slip, cranes, coal, fish quay, industry

### Takeaway
The certain 1967 features are: the station's **covered wooden pontoon** (replaced in 1986), the Lower Ferry slip at the south end of the South Embankment, the Boat Float under the road, the stone quay at Bayard's Cove, and Sandquay for the college. The rest is undocumented in what I could reach: fish quay, cranes, coal stores, gasworks and the exact river-boat landing. The 1986 flood-prevention scheme changed the Embankment's edge and pontoons.

### Cited Findings
- [V] The covered wooden station pontoon survived until the 1986 embankment flood-prevention works. — [Wikipedia, Dartmouth railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_railway_station)
- [V] The 1864 pontoon was linked to Spithead (the south side of the later Boat Float) by a bridge. — [Wikipedia, Dartmouth, Devon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth,_Devon)
- [V] The South Embankment ran from the **Lower Ferry Slip** to **Vavasour's Slip**. — [DHRG, A Brief History of Dartmouth](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/a-brief-history-of-dartmouth/)
- [V] (Modern) Dart Harbour lists the South Embankment as Dartmouth's "primary fish and working quay", with HGV access and davits for landing catch, plus a walk-ashore pontoon at Inside Town Jetty. — [Dart Harbour, Commercial](https://www.dartharbour.org/commercial-ships/)
- [U] "Until fairly recently a fish quay occupied the area where the boatyard and its associated marina are now". The walk does not make clear which bank this refers to. — [Explore Devon, The Dart Circuit](https://www.exploredevon.info/activities/walk/short-walks/the-dart-circuit/)
- [V] In the 1930s several coal firms had offices at 22 South Embankment. — [Steam Boat Trust, Portwey](https://www.thesteamboattrust.co.uk/portwey/project-one-ephnc-ghba6)
- [V] Mayor's Avenue keeps a warehouse with cargo hooks from the pre-1885 waterfront. — [SWCP walk 452](https://www.southwestcoastpath.org.uk/walksdb/452/printable/)
- [V] The River Dart Steamboat Co. ran Dartmouth–Totnes services after the war (MV *Berry Castle* acquired 1949; MV *Cardiff Castle* owned from 1964). Later the route passed to Dart Pleasure Craft ("River Link"). Today the river cruises leave from the **Dartmouth Town Jetty** on the Embankment. — [Wikipedia, River Dart Steamboat Co](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Dart_Steamboat_Co); [Wikipedia, MV Cardiff Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Cardiff_Castle); [Dart Harbour, Ferries](https://www.dartharbour.org/annual-berth-holders/local-information/ferries/)
- [V] Coombe Mud (now Coronation Park) was lined with shipyards and boatbuilding yards before the 1930s reclamation. — [Devon HCMTS (PDF)](https://dartmouthplan.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/J3-Devon-Historic-Coastal-and-Market-Towns-Survey.pdf); [Mayflower 400 trail](https://www.mayflower400uk.org/visit/dartmouth/attractions/dartmouth-heritage-trail/)

### Inferences
- The 1967 Embankment edge was probably lower and simpler, without the post-1986 flood wall, and had timber pontoons. Model the station pontoon as a roofed timber pontoon reached by a gangway.
- Commercial fishing (crab and shellfish) most likely landed on the South Embankment and in the Boat Float. This is not documented for 1967 specifically.

### Gaps
- **Gasworks** (location and whether it was working in 1967): no source found.
- **Cranes, coal stores and bunkering** in 1967: no source found beyond 1930s coal-firm offices.
- **Fish quay** location in 1967: unresolved.
- **River-boat (Totnes steamer) landing in 1967**: whether at the station pontoon, a separate North Embankment pontoon or the Town Jetty is not documented in what I reached.
- Slipways other than the Lower Ferry slip and Vavasour's Slip; North Embankment pontoons in 1967.

## 5. Street surfaces and furniture: cobbles, lamps, railings, benches, kiosks, pillar boxes, signs, parking

### Takeaway
Dartmouth keeps a set of **listed cast-iron lamp posts**, two of them at the Boat Float and called late 19th century by one source. It also has **listed K6 kiosks** on Victoria Road, at Coles Court and in the castle grounds. Cobbles are documented at Bayard's Cove and in the 1828 market yard. Whether the street lamps were gas or electric in 1967, and the details of signs, road markings and parking, were not found.

- The listed cast-iron columns (late 19th century) were certainly standing in 1967, so ornate cast-iron lamp columns around the Boat Float and Quay are correct. - K6 kiosks in GPO red were standard in 1967 (general knowledge, unverified for Dartmouth's exact positions then). The listed Victoria Road and Coles Court kiosks may well date from before 1967; their install dates were not found.
- Unverified general knowledge to confirm before use: pillar boxes in red with GR/VR/EIIR ciphers; Worboys road signs were introduced from 1965, so a mix of old and new signs would be plausible in 1967; road markings were white and yellow lines.

### Gaps

- Railings (Embankment and Boat Float), benches, and the dates and positions of pillar boxes.
- Road surfaces: which streets were tarmac and which setts in 1967.
- Parking arrangements on the Embankment and Quay in 1967, and the bus stops (vehicles are out of scope, but parking shapes the street scene).

## 6. The town's character in 1967: fishing, naval, tourist, yachting; population; summer season

### Takeaway
Dartmouth in the mid-1960s was a **small, self-contained borough of about 5,750 people**. DHRG describes it as resting on fishing and maritime trade, with a few tourist boats and the Royal Naval College. June comes before the August Royal Regatta. Its TV fame (*The Onedin Line*, 1971–80) and the marinas came later.

### Cited Findings
- [V] DHRG describes the mid-1960s town as "small and self-contained, based on fishing and maritime trade, with a few tourist boats and the Britannia Royal Naval College". — [DHRG, Dartmouth Then Dartmouth Now](https://dartmouth-history.org.uk/dhrg-books/dartmouth-then-dartmouth-now/)
- [V] 1961 census, Dartmouth parish: 2,730 males and 3,028 females, a total of **5,758** (my addition). The parish was formed in 1891 from St Saviour, Townstal and St Petrox, and appears to match the borough. — [Vision of Britain, Dartmouth CP (gender)](https://test.visionofbritain.org.uk/unit/10072347/cube/GENDER); [Vision of Britain, Dartmouth MB](https://test.visionofbritain.org.uk/unit/10042355)
- [V] Later populations: 5,512 in 2001 and 5,064 in 2011. — [Wikipedia, Dartmouth, Devon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth,_Devon)
- [V] The Royal Regatta is held annually in **August**. First recorded in 1822 and formally established in 1834, it became "Royal" after Queen Victoria and Prince Albert's unscheduled visit in 1856. — [Wikipedia, Port of Dartmouth Royal Regatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Dartmouth_Royal_Regatta); [Dartmouth Regatta history](https://www.dartmouthregatta.co.uk/history/)
- [V] *The Onedin Line* put Dartmouth on BBC television weekly from 1971 to 1980. — [Explore Dartmouth, film and TV](https://exploredartmouth.co.uk/dartmouth-film-tv-locations/)
- [V] (Modern) Dartmouth is "one of England's most important shellfish ports", with a fleet of small line-fishing boats. — [Dart Harbour, Commercial](https://www.dartharbour.org/commercial-ships/)
- Visual references to consult (not viewed): Britain from Above EPW024216, "The South Embankment, Boat Float and town, 1928" ([link](https://britainfromabove.org.uk/en/image/EPW024216)); BFI *From Paignton to Dartmouth* (1954) ([link](https://player.bfi.org.uk/free/film/watch-from-paignton-to-dartmouth-1954-online)); about 313 Francis Frith Dartmouth photographs, including "The Quay and Harbour 1949" ([link](https://www.francisfrith.com/us/dartmouth/photos)); British Pathé "Smith Brothers Cross Atlantic in Home-Made Yacht" (Dartmouth, undated) ([link](https://learningonscreen.ac.uk/newsonscreen/search/index.php/story/103061)).

### Inferences
- In June 1967 the town was in its early summer season: some visitors and river trippers, local fishing, naval cadets in town, and yachting on a smaller scale than today, since the marinas came from the 1970s (project SOURCES.md). The Regatta crowds and bunting belong to late August, not June.

### Gaps
- Visitor numbers, hotel and guest-house counts, and holiday-season patterns for the 1960s: not found.
- No 1960s-dated photograph, postcard or film of Dartmouth town was confirmed. The nearest dated visual sources found are from 1928, 1949, c.1950 and 1954. Kelly's Directories of the 1960s and the *Dartmouth Chronicle* were not reachable.
- The tools that failed (listed at the top) left the full Historic England list texts unread: the Boat Float with its bridge and railings, the station, St Saviour's tower, the Guildhall, the Butterwalk and the Pannier Market. Reading them directly, and the DHRG book's 1960s shop lists, are the highest-value next steps.
